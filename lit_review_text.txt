Abstract—This literature review synthesizes findings from sixty
peer-reviewed research papers spanning three critical medi-
cal imaging domains: gastric ulcer detection from endoscopic
images, pneumonia diagnosis from chest X-rays, and brain
tumor classification and segmentation from magnetic resonance
imaging (MRI). The review examines the methodologies, datasets,
performance metrics, and limitations reported across these do-
mains, with particular emphasis on deep learning and artificial
intelligence techniques. For gastric ulcer research, convolutional
neural networks (CNNs), object detection frameworks such as
YOLO variants, and attention mechanisms have demonstrated
diagnostic accuracies exceeding 96% in malignancy differen-
tiation tasks. In pneumonia detection, transfer learning with
architectures including VGG, ResNet, EfficientNet, and emerg-
ing Vision Transformers have achieved classification accuracies
ranging from 92% to 99.91%, with recent work addressing
explainability and federated learning for privacy preservation.
Brain tumor MRI analysis has seen extensive adoption of U-
Net variants for segmentation and ensemble models combining
ResNet and EfficientNet for classification, with reported accura-
cies surpassing 99% on benchmark datasets. Across all three
domains, common challenges persist: over-reliance on limited
public datasets, insufficient external validation, class imbalance,
and lack of prospective clinical trials. This review identifies
research gaps, compares methodological trends across domains,
and provides domain-specific conclusions on the maturity and
trajectory of AI-assisted diagnostic research.
Index Terms—Deep learning, medical image analysis, gastric
ulcer, pneumonia detection, brain tumor MRI, convolutional
neural networks, transfer learning, segmentation, classification.
I. INTRODUCTION
Medical imaging constitutes a cornerstone of modern clin-
ical diagnostics, enabling non-invasive visualization of in-
ternal pathologies across virtually every organ system. The
convergence of exponential growth in computational power,
availability of large-scale annotated datasets, and algorithmic
breakthroughs in deep learning has catalyzed a paradigm shift
in how medical images are interpreted. Convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) and their successors have demonstrated thecapacity to match—and in several controlled studies, exceed—
the diagnostic performance of trained specialists in tasks
ranging from retinal disease grading to pulmonary nodule
detection [1]–[3].
This literature review examines sixty research papers or-
ganized across three disease domains: (1) gastric ulcer and
gastric cancer detection from endoscopic and histopathological
images, (2) pneumonia diagnosis from chest X-ray (CXR)
and computed tomography (CT) images, and (3) brain tu-
mor classification and segmentation from magnetic resonance
imaging (MRI). These three domains were selected for joint
analysis because they collectively represent the breadth of AI-
driven medical image analysis—spanning gastrointestinal en-
doscopy, thoracic radiology, and neuro-oncological imaging—
while sharing fundamental methodological underpinnings in
deep learning architectures, transfer learning strategies, and
evaluation paradigms.
The gastric ulcer domain presents unique challenges due
to the visual similarity between benign and malignant ulcers
in endoscopic images, necessitating sophisticated feature ex-
traction to approximate the diagnostic acumen of experienced
gastroenterologists [4], [5]. Pneumonia detection from CXR
images has received heightened attention since the COVID-
19 pandemic, driving innovation in multi-class classification
frameworks that must distinguish viral, bacterial, and COVID-
19 pneumonia from normal lung images [7], [8]. Brain tumor
MRI analysis encompasses both classification of tumor types
(glioma, meningioma, pituitary) and pixel-level segmentation
for surgical planning, demanding architectures that balance
spatial precision with semantic understanding [3], [6].
The objectives of this review are threefold: (a) to system-
atically catalog the methodologies, datasets, and performance
metrics reported in the sixty source papers; (b) to synthesize
findings within each domain, identifying dominant approaches,
evolving trends, and persistent gaps; and (c) to draw cross-
domain comparisons that illuminate shared challenges and
transferable insights in AI-assisted medical diagnostics.
II. METHODOLOGY OF THEREVIEW
A. Source Material and Verification
The source material for this review comprises exactly sixty
research papers deposited in a designated input folder. Prior
to analysis, each PDF file was programmatically accessed
and its text content extracted using the PyMuPDF library.
All sixty files were confirmed accessible and parseable, with
no corrupted or unreadable files identified. The papers span
publication years from 2018 to 2026, reflecting the rapid
evolution of deep learning in medical imaging.
B. Categorization Criteria
Papers were categorized into three domains based on their
primary disease focus and imaging modality: (1) Gastric
Ulcer/Cancer—papers addressing endoscopic image analysis,
histopathological classification, or gastric microbiota profil-
ing (20 papers); (2) Pneumonia—papers addressing chest X-
ray or CT-based pneumonia detection, including COVID-
19 classification and systematic reviews (20 papers); and
(3) Brain Tumor MRI—papers addressing MRI-based brain
tumor classification, segmentation, or detection (20 papers).
Two papers were identified as potential duplicates (identical
title and content appearing under different filenames); these
are treated as a single work but both file entries are accounted
for in the inventory.
C. Analysis Framework
For each paper, the following elements were extracted:
research objective, dataset(s) used (including size and public
availability), methodology (model architecture, preprocessing,
training strategy), key performance metrics, reported strengths,
and acknowledged limitations. The synthesis within each
domain section follows a thematic organization—grouping
papers by methodological approach rather than presenting
individual summaries—to enable comparative analysis and
identification of trends.
III. GASTRICULCER ANDGASTRICCANCERDETECTION
A. Deep Learning for Endoscopic Image Classification
The application of deep learning to gastric endoscopic
image analysis has evolved from binary classification tasks to
complex multi-class and multi-task frameworks. Leeet al.[9]
proposed a computer-aided diagnosis (CADx) system using
Xception with depth-wise separable convolutions to classify
gastric cancer from pre-cancerous conditions including polyps,
ulcers, gastritis, and bleeding. Their preprocessing pipeline
incorporated Google’s AutoAugment for data augmentation
and SLIC superpixel segmentation with FRFCM clustering,
achieving an area under the ROC curve (Az) of 0.96 on biopsy-
confirmed ground truth.
Hatamiet al.[10] designed a custom CNN architecture
trained on 3,673 endoscopic images of gastric precancerous
diseases including erosion, polyps, and ulcers. Using data
augmentation, dropout, noise injection, and weight decayfor regularization, their model achieved 89% classification
accuracy—demonstrating that even moderately sized custom
architectures can provide clinically useful diagnostic support,
though the accuracy falls below that of deeper transfer learning
approaches.
The Gastro-AI model developed by Olimboyevet al.[11]
leveraged ResNet-50 with transfer learning on the HyperK-
vasir dataset—a comprehensive collection of 10,662 endo-
scopic images spanning 23 disease categories. Trained on
8,529 images and validated on 2,133, the model achieved
90.7% overall accuracy with sensitivity of 89.2% for diseased
cases and specificity of 92.1% for healthy tissue. Polyp de-
tection accuracy reached 94.2% and cancer detection 92.7%,
though inflammation detection was limited to 87.8% due to
image noise.
Kuchkorovet al.[12] developed a CNN system for auto-
matic detection of gastric ulcers and lesions in endoscopic
images, demonstrating that such systems could process large
volumes of stored endoscopic images rapidly with clinically
relevant diagnostic ability, potentially reducing endoscopist
workload.
Budhiraja and Batra [13] provided a review of deep learn-
ing approaches for gastric cancer detection and lesion seg-
mentation, comparing architectures including Mask R-CNN,
U-Net, GAS-Net, and GoogLeNet. Their analysis identified
GoogLeNet with Inception-v4 and transfer learning as the
highest-performing model at 96.7% accuracy.
B. Object Detection and Real-Time Lesion Localization
Object detection frameworks have been adapted for gastric
lesion localization, enabling real-time clinical decision support
during endoscopic procedures. Ahmadet al.[14] enhanced
the YOLOv7 algorithm through integration of a Squeeze-
and-Excitation (SE) attention block, significantly improving
small gastric lesion detection. The attention-powered YOLOv7
achieved precision of 0.72, recall of 0.69, F1-score of 0.71,
and mean average precision (mAP) of 0.71 at 63 frames
per second—representing a 10% mAP improvement over the
baseline YOLOv7.
Tanet al.[15] developed a multicenter, retrospective study
employing an improved YOLOv8 model with an illumination
attention module for real-time instance segmentation and clas-
sification of benign versus malignant gastric ulcers. Using a
dataset of 9,820 benign, 1,727 malignant, and 15,791 normal
mucosa images from four tertiary hospitals in China, the model
achieved overall precision, recall, and specificity of 0.91,
0.91, and 0.95 respectively. For malignant ulcer recognition,
specificity reached 0.99, demonstrating strong potential for
clinical deployment.
Kimet al.[16] developed a CNN model for multi-class
pathology lesion detection in gastric neoplasms using 10,181
white-light endoscopy images from 2,606 patients. Their six-
class categorization (EGC, AGC, dysplasia, BGU, benign
polyp, benign erosion) achieved a per-patient lesion detection
rate of 95.22%, with 100% detection for EGC and 97.22% for
AGC. Additionally, their T-stage estimation model achieved
85.17% accuracy for distinguishing T1 from T2–T4 cancers.
C. Malignancy Differentiation of Gastric Ulcers
Differentiating malignant from benign gastric ulcers remains
a core clinical challenge. Sunet al.[4] pioneered a CNN-
based gastric ulcer differentiation system using 854 endoscopic
images with biopsy-confirmed labels. They compared classi-
fication networks against detection network-based approaches
that first localized ulcer regions before classification, finding
that the detection-based method outperformed endoscopists’
diagnostic accuracy.
The same research group advanced this work through non-
local attention and densely-connected CNNs [5], incorporating
attention blocks into cascaded dense blocks to capture contex-
tual information and enhance pixel-region correlations. On a
gastroscopic dataset annotated twice per image by specialists,
they achieved 96.79% diagnostic accuracy, 94.92% recall,
and an F1-score of 94.70%, with AUC averaging 0.93—
approaching gastroenterologist-level performance.
Zhenget al.[17] proposed a mechanism mimicking gas-
troenterologists’ diagnostic behavior by integrating segmented
malignancy suspicious masks with gastroscopic images. A U-
Net network segmented suspicious pathological regions, which
then served as attention channels for a ResNet-based classifier.
Experiments on a dataset of over 900 patient cases demon-
strated substantial performance improvement over standard
classification using raw gastroscopic images alone.
Ramamoorthyet al.[18] proposed a Medical Diagnostic
Decision Support System (MDDSS) framework for early can-
cer diagnosis and ulcer classification in endoscopy videos,
utilizing a Flower Pollination algorithm for color feature
selection and achieving 97.56% efficacy for detecting and
classifying bleeding cancer and ulcer growth regions across
10,000 EGD datasets.
D. Domain-Specific Pre-Training and Transfer Learning
Leeet al.[19] introduced Gastro-BaseNet, a specialized
pre-trained model trained exclusively on gastroscopic image
data, addressing the domain gap between natural images (Im-
ageNet) and medical endoscopic images. Their comparative
study demonstrated that transfer learning from Gastro-BaseNet
consistently outperformed ImageNet-based transfer learning
for gastric cancer and ulcer prediction, with fine-tuning the
entire network yielding the highest performance. The Gastro-
BaseNet-based model also exhibited superior lesion localiza-
tion accuracy.
Yanget al.[20] developed a multi-task AI system capable of
simultaneously predicting gastric lesions,Helicobacter pylori
infection status, and lymph node metastasis from endoscopic
images, demonstrating the potential for comprehensive diag-
nostic support from a single AI platform.
Zubairet al.[21] presented a framework for gastric cancer
classification and segmentation using digital histopathology
images, employing an expectation-maximizing Na ¨ıve Bayes
classifier with a Gaussian Mixture Model. Their frameworkachieved classification accuracies of 98.87% and 97.28% on
two public datasets, with Grad-CAM visualizations enhancing
model interpretability.
E. Advanced Classification Approaches
Sabaet al.[22] introduced a novel approach combin-
ing CNN with Q-learning reinforcement for gastrointestinal
disease classification and attention-based U-Net for lesion
segmentation. The Q-learning component improved learning
stability through reward-based feedback, while the attention U-
Net highlighted important features for accurate segmentation.
Masmoudiet al.[23] presented a framework for automated
ulcer classification from wireless capsule endoscopy (WCE)
images using the KV ASIR dataset. ResNet50 and ResNet152-
V2 were trained on preprocessed data, features were fused
via concatenation, and optimized using a modified genetic
algorithm, achieving 99.67% classification accuracy.
Gnanapriyaet al.[24] proposed a system for identifying
gastric abnormalities with synchronous biopsy site localiza-
tion using CNN and UNet, providing dual functionality of
diagnosis and procedural guidance.
Yoon and Kim [25] reviewed lesion-based CNN approaches
for early gastric cancer diagnosis, discussing the design prin-
ciples for maximizing clinical usefulness when applying CNN
to specific organs and diseases.
F . Non-Imaging Clinical Studies
Sariet al.[26] conducted a microbiota profiling study of
98 Turkish patients, comparing gastric microbiota composition
between non-ulcer dyspepsia (NUD) and peptic ulcer disease
(PUD).H. pyloriDNA was detected in 71.4% of samples,
with significantly higher prevalence in PUD patients (82.6%
vs. 61.5%). The study identified oral-origin bacteria (Porphy-
romonas,Neisseria) that expanded opportunistically in the
absence ofH. pylori, providing microbiological context for
AI-based diagnostic research.
G. Trends and Gaps in Gastric Research
The gastric ulcer domain shows a clear trend from simple
binary classification toward comprehensive diagnostic systems
integrating detection, classification, staging, and even microbi-
ological prediction. Object detection frameworks (YOLO vari-
ants) are increasingly preferred for real-time applications. Key
gaps include: (1) limited multicenter validation—most studies
use single-institution datasets; (2) absence of prospective clin-
ical trials evaluating AI-assisted versus standard endoscopy;
(3) underexploration of video-based analysis compared to
single-frame approaches; and (4) insufficient integration of
clinical metadata (patient history, symptoms) with image-
based models.

B. Transfer Learning and Pre-Trained Models
Transfer learning has become the predominant approach
for pneumonia classification. Aliet al.[30] evaluated six
deep learning models on 5,856 CXR images, finding Efficient-
NetV2L achieved the highest accuracy at 94.02%, outperform-
ing CNN (87.78%), InceptionResNetV2 (88.94%), Xception
(90.7%), VGG16 (91.66%), and ResNet50 (87.98%).
Shilpaet al.[31] compared ResNet50, MobileNetV2,
AlexNet, EfficientNetB0, and Xception for pneumonia de-
tection, incorporating CLAHE preprocessing and cross-
validation. EfficientNetB0 achieved the highest test accuracy
of 99.78% with perfect precision, recall, and F1 scores,
demonstrating the importance of data preprocessing in achiev-
ing high performance.
Lamouadeneet al.[32] applied transfer learning using
ResNet18 combined with SVM on 21,165 CXR images,
achieving 98% accuracy with SGD optimization. Additional
models using EfficientNet-CNN and Xception-CNN achieved
99.20% and 98.80% accuracy respectively.
Agrawal and Gandhi [33] introduced PneumoSense++, a
framework comparing CNN, DenseNet121, and ResNet50
across seven optimizers (Adam, SGD, AdaGrad, AdaMax,
RMSprop, Nadam, AdaDelta) on 28,819 X-ray images.DenseNet121 with SGD achieved the highest performance:
94.80% accuracy, 97.69% recall, and 98.86% AUC.
C. Hybrid and Ensemble Methods
Hybrid approaches combining multiple architectures or
feature extraction strategies have shown promise. Ak-
shayet al.[34] proposed a dual-CNN feature fusion strategy
combining VGG16 and ResNet features, applying PCA for
dimensionality reduction retaining 95% variance, and classi-
fying using SVM and Random Forest—replacing the typical
CNN softmax with more interpretable ML classifiers.
Muniret al.[35] developed PneuX-Net, an ensemble
framework integrating Random Forest, Gaussian Na ¨ıve Bayes,
and K-Nearest Centroid. Using 10-fold cross-validation, the
PneuX-Net+KNC combination achieved 99.91% accuracy,
representing one of the highest reported figures in pneumonia
classification.
Hashmiet al.[36] presented a hybrid approach combining
ResNet-50 deep features with 2D-discrete wavelet transform
(DWT) and Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) hand-
crafted features, demonstrating that fusion of learned and
engineered features can enhance classification robustness.
D. Vision Transformers and Attention Mechanisms
The introduction of transformer architectures to medical
imaging represents a significant methodological shift. The
DA ViT model [37] proposed a Domain-Adapted Vision Trans-
former combining ViT self-attention for global feature capture
with shallow CNNs for local feature extraction. Evaluated on
5,856 CXR images, DA ViT achieved a 97% F1-score and
96% AUC for pneumonia detection, outperforming twelve
baseline methods. For pneumonia type classification (bacterial
vs. viral), DA ViT achieved 81% F1-score—significantly sur-
passing baselines by 25–74%. Grad-CAM heatmaps provided
interpretable visualizations.
Kunjumonet al.[38] developed a hybrid model utilizing
Swin Transformer’s self-attention and cross-attention mech-
anisms for hierarchical feature extraction, combined with
CNN-based classification. Residual connections and attention
mechanisms enhanced gradient flow and model convergence.
E. Federated Learning and Privacy-Preserving Methods
Rana and Marwaha [39] addressed data privacy through
a federated learning approach using autoencoder neural net-
works trained on decentralized CXR datasets. This unsuper-
vised learning paradigm enables multiple healthcare institu-
tions to collaboratively develop diagnostic models without
sharing raw patient data—a critical consideration for real-
world deployment.
F . Explainability and Interpretability
Renet al.[40] proposed an interpretable pneumonia detec-
tion system combining neural networks with Bayesian Net-
works on a dataset of 35,389 community-acquired pneumonia
cases. Their multi-source data approach improved performance
while providing intuitive explanations for diagnostic results,
addressing the critical “black box” limitation of deep learning
in clinical settings.
Li and Li [7] developed an AI deep learning platform using
voting algorithms combining multiple CNNs for COVID-19
pneumonia detection from 10,182 CXR images, achieving
99.95% diagnostic accuracy for COVID-19 among viral pneu-
monias.
G. Systematic Reviews and Comparative Studies
Khanet al.[1] conducted a systematic literature review
of intelligent pneumonia identification from CXR, analyzing
algorithm usability, computational complexity, and dataset
quality. Their findings indicated that deep learning algorithms
achieve 98.7% accuracy with 0.99 sensitivity and 0.98 speci-
ficity, while highlighting that the majority of applied datasets
are highly imbalanced and limited.
Rickardet al.[41] performed a scoping review of 35 studies
on ML-based viral versus bacterial pneumonia differentiation
in pediatric populations. Key findings include: 31 of 35 studies
used the Kermany dataset, raising overfitting concerns; median
binary classification accuracy was 92.3% (range: 80.8–97.9%);
and median multiclass accuracy was 91.8% (range: 76.8–
99.7%).
Aljuaidet al.[8] conducted a rigorous experimental com-
parison of six CNN architectures (ResNet, AlexNet, VGGNet,
SqueezeNet, DenseNet, InceptionV3) using a uniform pipeline
on 6,939 balanced CXR images. VGGNet achieved the highest
overall accuracy of 97%.
Hasanet al.[42] provided a comprehensive summary of
DL-based pneumonia diagnosis systems, analyzing datasets
and training methodologies across the field.
H. Trends and Gaps in Pneumonia Research
The pneumonia domain is characterized by: (1) over-
whelming reliance on the Kermany/Guangzhou pediatric CXR
dataset, limiting generalizability to adult populations and di-
verse imaging conditions; (2) a trend toward hybrid architec-
tures combining CNNs with transformers or traditional ML
classifiers; (3) growing emphasis on explainability through
Grad-CAM and Bayesian approaches; and (4) emerging in-
terest in privacy-preserving federated learning. Critical gaps
include the need for: multi-institutional prospective valida-
tion; standardized benchmarking protocols enabling fair model
comparison; exploration of CT-based pneumonia detection
(most studies use CXR); and integration of clinical variables
beyond imaging data.V. BRAINTUMORMRI CLASSIFICATION AND
SEGMENTATION
A. Transfer Learning for Tumor Classification
Transfer learning from ImageNet-pretrained models dom-
inates brain tumor classification research. Noreenet al.[6]
proposed multi-level feature extraction and concatenation us-
ing Inception-v3 and DenseNet201, extracting features from
different inception modules and dense blocks before concate-
nation. On a publicly available three-class dataset, the method
achieved 99.34% (Inception-v3) and 99.51% (DenseNet201)
testing accuracy.
Youniset al.[43] developed an enhanced ResNet50 model
with data augmentation and regularization for classifying
meningioma, glioma, pituitary, and no-tumor classes us-
ing 5,712 images. Class-specific accuracies reached 98.33%
(glioma), 94.44% (meningioma), 100% (no tumor), and 100%
(pituitary), with overall AUC of 0.999.
Asifet al.[46] conducted a systematic comparison of
Xception, NasNet Large, DenseNet121, and InceptionRes-
NetV2 with three optimizers (ADAM, SGD, RMSprop) on two
benchmark datasets. Their comprehensive analysis provided
guidance for selecting optimal architecture-optimizer combi-
nations.
Ahmad and Choudhury [47] evaluated seven transfer learn-
ing methods (VGG-16, VGG-19, ResNet50, InceptionRes-
NetV2, InceptionV3, Xception, DenseNet201) each followed
by five classifiers (SVM, Random Forest, Decision Tree,
AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting), achieving a best accuracy of
99.39% with 10-fold cross-validation.
Rizwanet al.[48] proposed a Gaussian Convolutional
Neural Network (GCNN) evaluated on two datasets—one for
three-class tumor classification (3,064 images from 233 pa-
tients) and another for glioma grade classification (516 images
from 73 patients)—achieving 99.8% and 97.14% accuracy
respectively.
Rehmanet al.[49] tested LeNET, MobileNetV2, DenseNet,
ResNet, and EfficientNet for brain tumor detection with
privacy preservation considerations for IoMT applications.
LeNET achieved the highest accuracy at 98.7%.
B. Segmentation Architectures
Brain tumor segmentation demands pixel-level precision.
Rajendranet al.[45] proposed combining U-Net and 3D CNN
predictions through various merging strategies, using GLCM
feature extraction for preprocessing. The combined approach
achieved 99.40% mean accuracy and 99.41% precision on the
BraTS dataset.
Tiwaryet al.[50] replaced UNet’s encoder with Effi-
cientNet, initially frozen to retain pre-trained features, while
preserving UNet’s bottleneck and skip connections. On the
Brain-Tumor.npy dataset (3,064 T1-weighted images from 233
patients), the model achieved 0.9925 accuracy.

Residual-Attention-UNet for multi-task brain tumor segmenta-
tion and classification. The Residual-Attention-UNet achieved

C. YOLO-Based Detection Methods
Almufarehet al.[44] compared YOLOv5 and YOLOv7 for
brain tumor detection and segmentation across three classes.
YOLOv5 achieved mAP of 0.947 at IoU 0.5 with precision
of 0.94, while YOLOv7 achieved 0.94 mAP with precision of
0.936. Both frameworks were systematically compared with
RCNN, Faster RCNN, and Mask RCNN.
D. Ensemble and Multi-Modal Approaches
Singhet al.[55] introduced an ensemble model combin-
ing ResNet50 (97.65% validation accuracy) and EfficientNet-
B7 (98.20%) for brain tumor classification on over 22,000
high-resolution MRI images. The ensemble achieved 99.68%
validation accuracy and 99.53% post-training accuracy—
exceeding both individual models.
Shyamala and Basha [56] proposed a multi-modal frame-
work using both MRI and CT images with AlexNet,
GoogleNet, SqueezeNet, and ResNet for feature extraction.
Classifier-level fusion with neural networks outperformed
SVM and KNN, with SqueezeNet-ResNet fusion achieving
near-perfect classification.
Ahmmedet al.[57] developed BTdiagAI, integrating fine-
tuned InceptionV3, DenseNet121, and Xception with ANOV A
and L1max feature selection. Their systematic evaluation of
five preprocessing methods identified CLAHE as optimal(99.8% accuracy on Dataset 1, 99.61% on Dataset 2), deploy-
ing results through a web platform for clinical use.
Majibet al.[58] proposed VGG-SCNet, a stacked classifier
combining multiple state-of-the-art transfer learning models,
achieving 99.2% precision, 99.1% recall, and 99.2% F1-score
for brain tumor detection.
E. Review Papers and Surveys
Solankiet al.[2] provided a comprehensive overview of
computational intelligence and statistical image processing
techniques for brain tumor detection, comparing classical ML
approaches with modern deep learning methods.
Karimet al.[3] investigated the landscape of brain tumor
segmentation using emerging deep learning methods, offering
comprehensive comparison while highlighting limitations re-
lated to data scarcity and validation robustness.
Jia and Chen [59] proposed FAHS-SVM using probabilistic
neural network classification for brain tumor detection, achiev-
ing 98.51% accuracy in distinguishing normal from abnormal
tissue in brain MRI.
F . Trends and Gaps in Brain Tumor Research
The brain tumor domain exhibits: (1) convergence to-
ward U-Net-based segmentation architectures, with attention
mechanisms and residual connections as standard enhance-
ments; (2) ensemble methods combining multiple pre-trained
architectures consistently outperforming individual models;
(3) growing interest in multi-task frameworks that jointly
perform classification, segmentation, and survival prediction.
Key gaps include: reliance on the Figshare/BraTS bench-
mark datasets limiting generalizability; insufficient exploration
of 3D volumetric analysis compared to 2D slice-based ap-
proaches; limited work on real-time intra-operative applica-
tions; and absence of multi-institutional prospective validation
studies.
VI. CROSS-DOMAINCOMPARISON
A. Imaging Modalities and Input Data
The three domains employ fundamentally different imaging
modalities. Pneumonia detection primarily uses chest X-rays
(2D grayscale projections), with a small subset exploring CT.
Brain tumor analysis uses MRI (multi-modal 3D volumes,
though most studies analyze 2D slices). Gastric ulcer research
uses endoscopic images (2D color images from direct visu-
alization) and occasionally histopathological slides. Despite
these differences, all three domains converge on similar deep

B. Dominant Architectures and Methodological Trends
TABLE IV summarizes the dominant architectures across
domains. ResNet variants (ResNet50, ResNet152) appear
across all three domains as either primary classifiers or fea-

in pneumonia detection. U-Net and its variants are almost

both gastric (YOLOv7, YOLOv8) and brain tumor (YOLOv5,

primarily in pneumonia detection (DA ViT, Swin Transformer),
representing an emerging trend that may spread to other
domains.
C. Evaluation Metrics and Validation Strategies
Accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC-ROC are
universally reported across all three domains. Brain tumor
segmentation additionally employs Dice coefficient and Jac-
card index. A concerning trend across all domains is the
predominance of single-dataset evaluation without external
validation. Cross-validation (typically 5-fold or 10-fold) is
more common in pneumonia research, while brain tumor and
gastric studies more frequently use train/validation/test splits.
D. Common Limitations
Several limitations recur across all three domains:
(1)Dataset homogeneity: most studies use a single public
dataset (Kermany for pneumonia, Figshare/BraTS for braintumors, custom single-institution datasets for gastric ulcers);
(2)Class imbalance: malignant cases are typically underrep-
resented relative to benign or normal classes; (3)Absence
of clinical deployment: very few studies report prospective
clinical trials or real-world deployment metrics; (4)Limited
demographic diversity: datasets are often drawn from single
geographic regions; and (5)Reproducibility concerns: many
studies do not release code or trained models.
VII. RESEARCHGAPS
A. Gastric Ulcer Domain
•Video-based analysis of full endoscopic procedures re-
mains underexplored compared to single-frame classifi-
cation.
•Integration of non-imaging biomarkers (microbiota pro-
files [26], serology) with AI image analysis is absent.
•Real-time, prospective clinical studies comparing AI-
assisted versus standard endoscopy have not been re-
ported.
•Multi-institutional dataset standardization for benchmark-
ing is lacking.
B. Pneumonia Domain
•Over-reliance on the Kermany pediatric dataset limits
generalizability to adult populations and diverse imaging
systems [41].
•Differentiation between viral subtypes (influenza, RSV ,
COVID-19) from CXR alone remains a substantial chal-
lenge.
•Federated learning approaches [39] require further matu-
ration and large-scale multicenter validation.
•Integration of clinical metadata (patient age, symptoms,
lab results) with image-based classification is minimal.
C. Brain Tumor MRI Domain
•True 3D volumetric segmentation is underrepresented;
most studies analyze individual 2D MRI slices.
•Real-time intra-operative tumor delineation for surgical
navigation has received limited attention.
•Survival prediction [54] is explored in very few studies
despite its clinical significance.
•Multi-modal integration (combining MRI with CT, PET,
or clinical data) remains nascent [56].
D. Cross-Domain Gaps
•Explainability techniques (Grad-CAM, SHAP, attention
maps) are applied inconsistently across domains.
•Standardized reporting frameworks for AI medical imag-
ing studies (analogous to CONSORT for clinical trials)
are needed.
•Transfer of architectural innovations between domains
(e.g., applying gastric endoscopy attention mechanisms
to CXR analysis) is unexplored.
•Regulatory and ethical frameworks for clinical deploy-
ment of these AI systems remain underdeveloped.
VIII. CONCLUSION
This literature review has synthesized findings from sixty
contemporary research papers across three critical domains
of AI-assisted medical image analysis. While each domain
presents unique challenges related to imaging modalities and
diagnostic objectives, several overarching themes emerge re-
garding the maturity, limitations, and future trajectory of the
field.
A. Gastric Ulcer: Key Takeaways
The gastric ulcer domain has progressed from basic binary
(benign/malignant) classification to comprehensive diagnostic
systems incorporating detection, localization, multi-class clas-
sification, and staging estimation. Dominant methods include
CNN-based classification (ResNet, Xception, DenseNet), ob-
ject detection frameworks (YOLOv7, YOLOv8), and attention
mechanisms for focusing on diagnostically relevant regions.
The field is approaching clinical readiness for specific tasks—
malignancy differentiation accuracy exceeds 96% in controlled
studies [5], [15]—but remains immature in terms of prospec-
tive clinical validation, multicenter generalizability, and inte-
gration with clinical workflows. Domain-specific pre-training
(Gastro-BaseNet [19]) represents a promising direction that
may generalize to other medical imaging domains.
B. Pneumonia: Key Takeaways
Pneumonia detection from CXR represents perhaps the most
extensively studied of the three domains, driven by the avail-
ability of large public datasets and the urgency of the COVID-
19 pandemic. Transfer learning with architectures such as
EfficientNet, VGG, and ResNet achieves consistently high
accuracy (94–99.9%), with ensemble and hybrid approaches
reaching the performance ceiling on existing benchmarks.
The field is transitioning toward: (a) Vision Transformer-
based architectures offering global context [37], [38]; (b) ex-
plainable AI frameworks [40] providing clinical transparency;
and (c) privacy-preserving federated learning [39]. However,
the field’s maturity is tempered by the critical observation
that most high-accuracy results derive from a single dataset
(Kermany), and performance on diverse real-world populations
remains unvalidated [1], [41].C. Brain Tumor MRI: Key Takeaways
Brain tumor MRI analysis has achieved exceptional classi-
fication accuracy (exceeding 99% on standard benchmarks)
through ensemble transfer learning and dedicated segmen-
tation architectures. U-Net variants enhanced with attention
mechanisms and EfficientNet encoders represent the state of
the art for segmentation, while ensemble models combin-
ing complementary architectures (ResNet50 + EfficientNet-
B7 [55]) define the classification frontier. The domain exhibits
the greatest methodological depth, with studies addressing
classification, segmentation, detection, and survival prediction
within unified frameworks [54]. The primary maturity bottle-
neck is the transition from benchmark performance to clinical
deployment—the gap between 99%+ accuracy on curated
datasets and reliable performance in diverse real-world clinical
settings remains substantial. Multi-modal integration [56] and
web-deployed diagnostic tools [57] represent emerging efforts
to bridge this gap.
